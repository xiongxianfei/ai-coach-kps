# Constructed adult learning example

## Key takeaway

An inspectable example can clarify outputs without inventing a completed learner session.

They show possible applications of the master Practice, not required topics, lesson lengths, question counts or teaching sequences. All responses and outcomes described as fictional remain illustrations, not learner evidence or demonstrations of educational effectiveness.

## Target and reference relation

Target: compare two quantities on a common basis and justify the calculation. The constructed rule is unit cost = price / quantity, with quantities expressed in the same units. No external shopping decision or financial recommendation is being made.

For A, $6 divided by 750 g is $0.008 per g, or $0.80 per 100 g. For B, $7.20 divided by 1,000 g is $0.0072 per g, or $0.72 per 100 g. Total price alone does not answer this constructed per-unit question.

## From subject panorama to one first task

A **constructed panorama** locates unit-price reasoning in quantitative reasoning: arithmetic, measurement and units, proportional relationships and data interpretation. The current goal is one narrower relation—compare amounts on a common quantity basis—rather than mastering those entire regions. The [two-level planning Practice](../practices/concept-mapping-and-instructional-sequencing-with-ai.md) proposes conversion, unit rate and integrated comparison as *possible* learning stages. For the first learning task, it selects only one output:

| Task card field | Constructed first task |
|---|---|
| Goal | Recognize equivalent quantities so a later rate comparison can use common units |
| Task | Explain whether 0.75 kg and 750 g denote the same quantity, using the declared unit relation |
| Observable output | A short explanation of equivalence in your own words |
| Criterion | Explicitly identify that 1 kg equals 1000 g and the value of the quantity is preserved |
| Allowed support | An accessible text display; verified explanation on request is recorded as instructional assistance |
| Stop or split | After one response and checking, proceed to a rate task, teach the missing unit relation, or pause |

This is a proposed Day 1 *example*, not an actual day, interview, test or observed learning result. It is small without being mere disconnected vocabulary. The broader plan may have other stages, but they are not silently assigned as additional Day 1 requirements. See [One Learning Task at a Time](learning-workflow.md).

## Connected lesson example

This constructed example shows how feedback can motivate the next lesson within a stage about relating linguistic structure, meaning and contextual evidence. Every response and note below is fictional, not a transcript or learner result. Lesson numbers indicate an illustrative sequence, not days or a required teaching format. Its topic, length, two-question set, use of contrasts and order are choices for this scenario. A different subject or learner may need a demonstration, discussion, direct explanation, practical activity or a different route; preserve the meaning and evidence boundaries rather than copying this sequence. The word analysis follows [Richards, *More morphology*, numbered pages 16–18](../references/richards-2022-morphology.md); the situations, inference checks and coaching decisions are authored applications. This source does not establish interpretation preferences or coaching effectiveness.

### Lesson 1 Connect word structure with meaning

The capability is to explain two analyses of *unlockable*, including what each part contributes. A base is the expression an affix attaches to. Here, one *un-* attaches to a verb and reverses its action; another attaches to an adjective and negates its property. *-able* combines with a verb to express possibility. Build the interpretation from the inside outward:

| Structure | Contributions | Resulting interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| `[[un + lock] + able]` | Form the verb *unlock*, then apply *-able* | Can be unlocked |
| `[un + [lock + able]]` | Form *lockable*, then negate that property | Cannot be locked |

The letters alone do not display this grouping. For a working door that can be locked and then opened with its key, the first interpretation applies and the second does not. Calling the first interpretation true therefore does not make the second true; these are different claims about what can be done.

An application task asks the learner to explain both groupings and consider this report: “The caretaker called the door unlockable. She was carrying a key.” Does the report establish which meaning she intended? The teaching table remains available, so this is supported application, not a hidden-answer baseline. In live coaching, present the task and wait before feedback.

**Fictional response for illustration:** “With un- on lock, it means reverse locking, then able means possible. With un- on lockable, it means not possible to lock. Because she carries a key, she definitely means it can be unlocked.”

The response connects the parts to both meanings under the available support. The word *definitely* goes beyond the evidence: carrying a key does not establish that it operates this door or that the caretaker intended that reading. This is a localized inference problem, not evidence that all the morphological analysis needs reteaching.

### Feedback and the next lesson choice

A possible coach reply is:

> Your two explanations connect the groupings to their meanings correctly. Carrying a key makes the unlocking interpretation worth considering, but it does not establish the caretaker's intention: the key could belong to another door. The unresolved question is how to distinguish an available meaning from evidence about a particular situation. We will use the two meanings you just explained to examine that distinction next.

The first lesson's supported word-analysis task can be recorded as meeting its local criterion in this fictional case. Contextual inference remains open; the correction is teaching, not a new successful learner response. The next lesson directly develops that open relationship, so no routine permission question or duplicate test of both definitions is needed. If the original response instead confused the meanings, repair that distinction before using it as a premise. Other observed gaps would remain noted separately.

### Lesson 2 Distinguish meanings from contextual evidence

The central question is: **What does each meaning claim, and what does the available evidence actually establish?** Three things must stay distinct: the interpretations made available by the word structure, whether an interpretation fits the described situation, and what a particular speaker intended.

Start with a concrete case. A door's mechanism engages when locked, and the correct key releases it. The description supports both the ability to lock it and the ability to unlock it. Consequently, “can be unlocked” fits, while “cannot be locked” conflicts with the stated facts. Knowing that one reading fits the facts still differs from directly observing a speaker's intention; a speaker might be mistaken or using a different context.

Now change one condition: the mechanism is broken so that the door cannot be secured by locking. “Cannot be locked” fits this description. That fact alone does not tell us whether some already engaged lock can be released. The two readings answer different questions; do not infer one capability merely by reversing the truth of the other description.

Return to the caretaker carrying a key. Unlike the explicit facts about the mechanism, this observation leaves important information unspecified. It gives a possible reason to consider unlocking, but the key might be unrelated. A direct clarification such as “I mean that the door cannot be locked” would explicitly identify the intended reading, though it would not by itself verify the mechanism's physical condition.

The relationship is therefore: **word structure supplies the two analyses; the stated facts let us check their application; additional context can inform an interpretation without automatically settling intention or factual truth.** This is a bounded explanatory comparison, not a claim that language processing always follows three successive steps. Its wording and representations can be adapted without losing these distinctions.

### Complementary application and the wait boundary

After the explanation and any clarification, present two short tasks together:

1. A cabinet can be locked and its correct key opens it. Which reading of *unlockable* fits these facts, and which conflicts with them? Give one brief reason.
2. A technician calls another cabinet “unlockable” while carrying a replacement lock. Does that establish the intended meaning? Name one short clarification that would distinguish the readings.

The first task checks applying the meanings to explicit facts; the second checks the limit of circumstantial evidence. If an existing response already establishes one distinction, omit its duplicate. Further questions are justified by remaining uncertainty, not by a fixed quota.

**Stop here in live delivery and wait for the actual reply.** There is no fictional second response or automatic lesson-completion claim.

For author review only: task 1 supports “can be unlocked” and rules out “cannot be locked” under the stated facts. Task 2 does not establish the intention; the replacement may concern this cabinet or another, and a clarification can ask which of the two meanings the technician intends. Preserve both the answer and actual assistance. These keys are visible in the published example; use fresh, checked material if an unexposed assessment is needed. Success with cabinets would support a bounded application, not general competence with every kind of linguistic ambiguity or delayed retention.

## HTTP lesson and partial progress

This optional example applies the master Practice to **tracing an HTTP request and bounding conclusions from its response**. The pseudocode, example outputs and learner reply are constructed; no application was launched, no learner was assessed and no private learning record is reproduced. The lesson may remain a coherent whole with grouped questions. Its command choices and answer formats are illustrative, not requirements for other lessons.

### Make the processing visible

A router selects application code using information such as the method and path. A handler performs the selected operation and constructs a response. The following deliberately simplified pseudocode defines this example's behavior; it is neither executable lab software nor a description of an unseen application. `respond` constructs a status, headers and body; `form_decode` converts the form body into fields; `json_encode` serializes the result as JSON text.

```text
if method == GET and path == /welcome:
    respond(200, text/plain, "Welcome")
else if method == GET and path == /begin:
    respond(302, Location=/welcome, empty body)
else if method == POST and path == /mirror:
    fields = form_decode(request_body)
    result = {message: fields["message"]}
    respond(200, application/json, json_encode(result))
else if method == GET and path == /private:
    respond(403, text/plain, "Forbidden")
else:
    respond(404, text/plain, "Not found")
```

For the worked trace, assume a valid form body containing `message=good+morning`, with `+` decoded as a space. Invalid bodies, missing fields and production error handling are outside this illustration.

1. The client chooses `POST /mirror` and supplies the form body. The message is client input.
2. The method and path select the mirror handler. Merely reaching the server does not select every handler.
3. The decoder yields the field value `good morning`; the handler places that value in a result object.
4. JSON serialization produces `{"message":"good morning"}`. The response reports status 200 and content type `application/json`.
5. The client can inspect the returned status, headers and body. The supplied pseudocode explains the intended internal steps; a matching response alone would not identify a particular parser or implementation.

The `/private` branch is especially useful for the evidence boundary: it emits 403 without consulting any identity or permission data. A refusal response alone therefore cannot demonstrate a functioning authorization policy. An unmatched request also receives a response in this example; 404 does not mean the application failed to process it. More generally, HTTP 404 can conceal a resource's existence, so it does not uniquely identify a missing route. [RFC 9110, sections 15.5.4–15.5.5](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html#section-15.5.4) supplies these status semantics; the branch logic is authored here.

### Keep the task answerable

If using a real local lab, first provide or confirm its files, launch instructions and relevant route behavior. This guide supplies no `app.py`. Prepared exchanges can support a conceptual lesson when a lab is unavailable; label them supplied examples and leave practical execution unassessed. Explain unfamiliar commands briefly rather than making command discovery an accidental prerequisite.

For a GET redirect comparison, curl's `-i` includes response headers and `-L` follows redirects. Optional `-v` exposes outgoing request headers as well. `--data-urlencode` supplies encoded data and ordinarily selects POST when no other method-changing option is used. These options are tools for inspecting the exchange, not the learning objective. [Official curl manual](https://curl.se/docs/manpage.html) documents their behavior; this example does not claim to have executed them.

For this lesson, a compact set could request:

| Task | What to provide |
|---|---|
| Compare `GET /welcome` and `GET /absent` | Their status codes and one brief explanation using the supplied routing logic |
| Trace `GET /begin` with and without following the redirect | The status sequence and what additional request following requires |
| Send a different message to `/mirror`, or trace a supplied exchange | The submitted field, returned value and the transformation shown by the pseudocode |
| Interpret `/private` | What the response reports and whether the supplied code checks permissions |

Relevant output plus a short explanation is enough; do not request full logs or essays unless they serve the target capability. Accept equivalent response formats. Use a different message for application, and distinguish a prediction from actual execution. The worked solution is visible, so these tasks do not constitute an unexposed baseline. If the learner has already demonstrated one distinction, reuse that evidence rather than repeat its question.
