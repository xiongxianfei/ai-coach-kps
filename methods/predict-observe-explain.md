# Predict Observe Explain

## Key takeaway

Record an expectation and its reason, inspect what happens, then explain how the result relates to that expectation.

## Summary

Predict–Observe–Explain (POE) makes the relationship between a learner's expectation, evidence and explanation available for discussion. It needs an event, demonstration or investigation with an inspectable result. A prediction question alone is still useful, but it is not the complete procedure.

## Rationale and what the sequence makes visible

A correct forecast can come from a sound account, remembered wording or a guess. An initial reason helps distinguish those possibilities. The observation supplies something to compare with that account; the final explanation shows whether the learner can connect the two. Neither agreement nor disagreement alone establishes understanding. This is the rationale for examining the three contributions separately, not a claim that the sequence guarantees learning.

## Inputs and prerequisites

Choose a relationship the learner has enough background to reason about, and make the setup clear without disclosing the particular outcome. Check that the result can actually be inspected with the available materials. A real event, a recording or an executable simulation can supply observations, but they support different claims: a simulation shows the behavior of its implemented rules, not automatically the physical world.

## Procedure

The following procedure summarizes the inspected POE guidance:

1. Describe the event and ask for an expected outcome with a reason. Preserve that response before revealing the result.
2. Carry out or show the demonstration. Let the learner inspect it and report what happened.
3. Compare the report with the initial expectation. Ask for an explanation of the result, including any discrepancy, and discuss or revise the account.

Written sentences are not essential: a spoken explanation, choice with a reason or labelled drawing may fit. A surprising outcome is not required. These options and the observation requirement are discussed in the [NZCER guidance](../references/joyce-2006-predict-observe-explain.md).

## Worked example with equal sharing

This is an authored illustration using twelve counters, not an observed learner session or a source experiment. It assumes the learner understands equal groups and can count the counters.

First arrange twelve counters into three equal groups. Each group has four. Show this starting state, then say that the same twelve counters will be rearranged into six equal groups.

Before rearranging, ask for the expected number in each new group and a brief reason. Do not show the table below as the answer before the prediction in a live activity. The learner may use their understanding of grouping; an unsupported guess should not be interpreted as demonstrated reasoning.

Then rearrange the counters and let the learner count. The checked result for the author is:

| Arrangement | Total counters | Number of groups | Counters per group |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starting state | 12 | 3 | 4 |
| After rearrangement | 12 | 6 | 2 |

An adequate explanation connects the fixed total with the increased number of equal groups: doubling the groups halves each share, because `6 × 2 = 12`. “There are two” reports the result but does not yet explain it. “More groups means more in each” conflicts with the fixed total. These are possible response interpretations, not invented learner answers.

If the learner counts three counters in each of six groups, inspect the setup together: that would require eighteen counters. Repair an ambiguous or incorrectly executed demonstration before treating the report as a conceptual difficulty. If the physical materials are unavailable, a supplied table can support interpretation, but it is supplied evidence, not a learner-performed experiment.

## Output checks and what follows

Preserve the original prediction, observed or supplied evidence, final explanation and assistance. A changed answer with no reason may reflect answer copying. A consistent reason and outcome supports understanding of this case; it does not establish retention or independent performance elsewhere. If the terms or setup remain unclear, explain them rather than asking for repeated predictions.

The [coaching Practice](../practices/ai-assisted-learning-orchestration.md#stage3-select-one-current-learning-task) owns live selection and pacing. Group questions that can be answered from the current information, while withholding a later observation until its prediction has been recorded. If a learner prefers an uninterrupted reading exercise, offer a supplied-case analysis and describe it accurately instead of calling it an independent POE attempt.

## Limits and alternatives

Use a simpler prediction question when checking a consequence is enough. Use a worked demonstration when essential background is missing, or an application task when the goal is performing the action. POE does not require every lesson to become an experiment. The cited classroom study investigated perceptions and discussion rather than causal conceptual-change effects; adult AI coaching and this arithmetic illustration were not tested there.
