# Structured Explanation

## Key takeaway

Build an explanation whose terms, relationships and examples let the learner follow why a conclusion holds and where it stops applying.

## Summary

Structured Explanation is the descriptive name used here for an authored adaptation of university lecture guidance. It is not the title of an established protocol. Carnegie Mellon's Eberly Center provides the procedural basis: orient the learner, connect the exposition, develop examples with their limits, and provide synthesis and participation. The implementation below adapts those choices to a readable lesson in conversation.

## Inputs and prerequisites

A scoped learning capability, accurate subject material, the learner's relevant starting knowledge, and a question or phenomenon worth explaining. Check the content before making it persuasive. An analogy, confident tone or polished diagram cannot establish its truth.

## Rationale

Naming a relationship leaves the explanatory work undone. The learner needs a bridge from familiar ideas to the new conclusion. A worked contrast makes that bridge inspectable: what changed, what stayed fixed, and why did the result differ or remain the same?

## Procedure and authored adaptation

Choose a meaningful entry point and explain enough context to make the question intelligible. Introduce unfamiliar terms where they become necessary. Develop the relationship in connected prose, using a demonstration, comparison or representation when it makes the reasoning easier to inspect. State the conditions under which the account applies and reconnect the result to the lesson's purpose.

For conversation, prepare this as a coherent teaching passage before the selected assessment questions when that matches the learner's preference. Invite clarification without making every paragraph depend on another reply. These pacing choices are local adaptations; the university source also recommends interaction during lectures. Neither its classroom timings nor a fixed attention span is adopted here.

The content determines the order. A useful explanation can begin with a case, a problem, a mechanism or a surprising contrast. It need not label everything a Principle or use a fixed list of subsections.

## Worked explanatory passage

**Constructed example for a learner who knows addition and division:**

> An average can describe a collection without describing each member. Imagine three boxes containing 1, 4 and 7 counters. There are 12 counters altogether. If we redistributed them equally, each box would contain 4. That equal-share amount is the arithmetic mean: 12 divided by 3.
>
> Now compare three boxes that already contain 4 counters each. The total and number of boxes are unchanged, so the mean is still 4. What changed is the distribution across boxes. The first collection varies; the second does not. Reporting only “mean 4” discards that difference.
>
> Therefore, the mean alone cannot establish that every box contains 4 counters. It does not even require any box to contain exactly the mean: boxes containing 2 and 6 have mean 4. This account uses an ordinary, equally weighted arithmetic mean; other summaries and weighting rules answer different questions.

This passage defines the unfamiliar idea through an operation, explains an invariant and demonstrates a limit. The counters are an illustration, not an experiment or reported learner result. A diagram would be useful if counting or redistribution remained unclear; it is not required merely to decorate the explanation.

## Output and validation

The output is the explanation itself, not instructions telling a future coach to explain. Hide its links and inspect whether a reader has enough information to interpret the worked contrast. Check each example against the claimed relationship and look for an unstated condition. Read the planned questions next: they should ask for reasoning the lesson actually prepares, with any new context introduced clearly.

If a learner repeats the wording but cannot interpret a changed case, the explanation may not yet support usable understanding. Inspect the actual response before deciding whether the cause is a missing prerequisite, an unclear example or a poorly framed question. Add the missing bridge rather than automatically adding more prose.

## Limits and alternatives

Exposition can introduce or connect knowledge; it cannot demonstrate a learner's physical performance or independent application. Use [worked-example instruction](worked-example-instruction.md) when solving a task is central. Use [cognitive modeling](cognitive-modeling.md) when the important missing content is an expert's choice or check. A familiar topic may need a concise answer, while a genuine investigation may call for inquiry. These options can be combined without making each mandatory.
