# Self Explanation

## Key takeaway

The learner generates an explanation connecting studied information to its meaning or justification.

## Summary

This Method explains the learner's operation. The [coaching Practice](../practices/self-regulated-learning-with-ai.md#stage3-agree-on-assistance-and-begin-instruction) owns when to select it, assistance and next-action decisions.

## Inputs and prerequisites

Prepare material with a relationship worth explaining and enough context to interpret its terms. For the arithmetic illustration below, that means knowing what a total, an equal group and multiplication represent. No specialist knowledge about learning research is needed to follow the example. An unfamiliar term can be clarified before it becomes an unexplained premise.

## Rationale

Chi and colleagues propose that self-explanation constructs inferences, integrates new information with prior knowledge and allows incremental revision. These are explanatory accounts, not a guaranteed causal pathway. Incorrect inferences are possible. [Discussion, printed pages 470–472](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Chi%20et%20al%20-%201994%20-%20Eliciting%20self-explanations%20improves%20understanding.pdf).

In the example below, the useful addition is the connection between division and equal groups. Merely reporting the quotient leaves that connection unstated. This distinction explains what to inspect in this example; it supplies no evidence that the activity improves every learner's performance.

## Procedure

The inspected text-learning procedure can be summarized as follows:

1. Read a sentence.
2. Explain its meaning, connections with earlier material, and questions it raises.
3. Clarify vague statements when prompted; reread where needed.
4. Continue through the material.

The study also used selected component-function questions. [Procedure, pages 450–451; Appendix, page 477](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Chi%20et%20al%20-%201994%20-%20Eliciting%20self-explanations%20improves%20understanding.pdf). Sentence-by-sentence pacing is this study's implementation, not a compulsory chat rhythm.

## Worked application

This is a constructed arithmetic illustration, not a participant response or a replication of the text-learning experiment.

The studied step is: **Twelve tokens shared equally among three groups gives four tokens per group: `12 ÷ 3 = 4`.** The equal-sharing condition matters: four is each group's allocation only when the shares are equal.

A possible learner-generated explanation is:

> Three counts the groups. Giving one token to each group uses three tokens each round. Four rounds use all twelve tokens, so each group gets four. Multiplying three groups by four tokens per group reconstructs twelve tokens.

The explanation connects the divisor to the situation, gives a reason for the quotient and checks it against the total. It is more informative about this relationship than merely saying “divide twelve by three.” If the allocation is unequal, twelve divided by three still gives the average, but does not establish what each group received.

The reader can inspect the operation in this example: begin with the studied calculation, identify what each quantity represents, connect the operation to equal sharing, and check whether the proposed explanation reconstructs the stated total. This is an illustration of the Method, not a requirement that every explanation use arithmetic or a reconstruction check.

## Output and validation

The output is an explanation to inspect, not an automatic correctness score. For the constructed example, distinguish these responses:

| Response | What it establishes or leaves open |
|---|---|
| “Twelve divided by three is four.” | Restates the calculation; the reason for division remains unstated. |
| “Three is the number of equal groups, and each gets four.” | Interprets the quantities; reconstructing the total can make the justification explicit. |
| “Division always makes a number smaller.” | Offers a rationale, but a false generalization: `12 ÷ 0.5 = 24` is a counterexample. |
| “I do not know what the three counts.” | Identifies a specific unresolved connection. |

These are authored interpretations of invented responses, not a validated scoring rubric. Compare the actual explanation with the subject's definitions, assumptions and a checked example or source. A longer answer can still contain the same error. Preserve uncertainty when the subject claim cannot be checked. The coaching Practice explains how to choose clarification, instruction or another task from that evidence.

## Distinguishing nearby activities

For this example, reading the supplied explanation gives access to someone else's reasoning. Restating the calculation supplies its wording or result. Generating the equal-sharing justification supplies the learner's account of the relationship. These can all have a place in a lesson; their outputs support different interpretations.

Retrieving a previously studied explanation with the answer hidden asks whether it can be recalled. Explaining while the material is visible asks for reasoning with that material available. The activities can overlap, so record actual conditions instead of inferring independence from the method's name. A correct explanation of this example alone does not establish later recall or successful application elsewhere.

## Limits

The study concerned eighth graders learning a circulatory-system text; study times differed and post-tests allowed text and notes. It does not validate adult AI coaching. [Procedure, pages 450–451](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/Chi%20et%20al%20-%201994%20-%20Eliciting%20self-explanations%20improves%20understanding.pdf).

Do not treat a fluent explanation as a substitute for checking its premises. Where the necessary terms or relationships are unavailable, repeated requests to explain do not supply that missing information. Choosing prerequisite instruction, an accessible response format or a stopping point is a contextual Practice decision. This document does not prescribe a universal prompt, explanation length, question count or superiority over demonstration and retrieval.
