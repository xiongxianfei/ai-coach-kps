# Backward Design

## Key takeaway

Plan toward an intended capability, decide what evidence would demonstrate it, then design the learning that makes it attainable.

## Summary

Backward Design is the planning approach in McTighe and Wiggins's Understanding by Design framework. Its three decisions connect desired results, acceptable evidence and instruction. This document applies that approach to a bounded adult lesson; it does not reproduce the full framework or validate an AI course.

## Inputs and prerequisites

A meaningful learner goal, credible subject coverage, available evidence of prior understanding, and practical constraints. Unknown learner knowledge stays unknown. Consult disciplinary knowledge before narrowing the goal to whatever is easiest to test.

## Rationale

A topic list names material; a capability states what the learner should be able to do with it. Planning the evidence exposes a mismatch such as teaching definitions while expecting an unfamiliar analysis. It also reveals prerequisites that the explanation must supply.

## Procedure

1. Identify the intended understanding and use, including relevant knowledge and boundaries.
2. Decide what learner performance would support that result and how it would be interpreted.
3. Plan the explanations, demonstrations and learner work needed to prepare for that performance. Revisit the alignment when actual responses reveal a gap.

These are design decisions, not mandatory learner-facing headings. Planning assessment before instruction does not mean giving the test before teaching. The authors focus on units rather than isolated lesson plans. Establish the broader stage goal first, then locate the lesson within it. A transfer goal may need several lessons and later evidence; each lesson need not contain a culminating performance.

## Worked planning example

**Constructed adult lesson:** A learner can calculate an arithmetic mean but treats it as a description of every individual.

The proposed capability is to explain what a mean does and does not tell us about individual values. The necessary coverage includes equal-share interpretation, variation and the loss of detail when a collection is summarized. Formal probability is outside this lesson.

Evidence could be a comparison of two small collections with the same mean and an explanation of whether every value must equal it. Correct arithmetic alone would not establish the intended distinction. An open-note response can demonstrate reasoning with support; it cannot establish unaided recall.

Prepare a complete explanation of the mean as total divided by count. Work through `[4, 4, 4]` and `[1, 4, 7]`: each totals 12, contains three values and has mean 4, but their individual values differ. Then explain why redistributing a fixed total can preserve the mean. Give a fresh comparison rather than asking the learner to repeat those numbers.

If the learner cannot calculate either mean, clarify the calculation before judging the intended comparison. If they calculate correctly but infer equal individual values, revisit the counterexample. If they explain both, a later case with an unequal number of observations can examine whether they can apply the idea beyond this example. These are authored continuation choices, not a compulsory sequence.

## Output and validation

The output is a usable lesson or course plan with a defensible connection between its goal, evidence and teaching. Read the planned task against the actual explanation: could the learner attempt it using the knowledge supplied, without an unexplained term or operation? Also check whether success would support the intended capability or merely a convenient proxy.

A plan can pass that author check and still teach poorly. Learner responses, reported confusion and later application can justify revision. They do not by themselves isolate the causal effect of this planning method.

## Limits and alternatives

Use this when designing or substantially revising instruction. A short clarification during an established lesson may need only a direct explanation. Avoid turning every response into a new design form. Keep broader subject coverage visible so that choosing assessable goals does not quietly delete important knowledge.
